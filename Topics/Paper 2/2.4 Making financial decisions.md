# 2.4 Making financial decisions

> [!info] Spec reference
> Topic 2.4 covers business performance — gross profit, net profit, GP margin, NP margin (2.4.1), and average rate of return (ARR) for investment appraisal. Plus understanding business performance through financial data and the limitations of financial information (2.4.2). **Profit/margin calc appears every year**; ARR has appeared in 2019, 2022, 2024.

## What this topic covers
- **Sources of finance for an established business**: retained profit, share capital (Ltd/plc), loan capital, selling assets — and their trade-offs at scale
- **Gross profit, net profit** and the corresponding **margin** ratios
- **Average rate of return (ARR)** for comparing investment options
- **Interpreting** financial statements (P&L extracts) to comment on performance
- **Limitations** — figures are historic, ignore qualitative factors, can hide cash issues

## Key terms
- **Gross profit**: sales revenue − cost of sales
- **Cost of sales**: direct cost of producing what you sold (raw materials, factory labour)
- **Net profit**: gross profit − other operating expenses and interest
- **Gross profit margin (%)**: (gross profit / sales revenue) × 100
- **Net profit margin (%)**: (net profit / sales revenue) × 100
- **Average rate of return (ARR)**: (average annual profit / cost of investment) × 100
- **Retained profit**: profit kept inside the business (free finance, no interest, no dilution)
- **Share capital**: money raised by issuing new shares (no repayment but dilutes ownership)
- **Loan capital**: long-term borrowing repaid with interest (keeps ownership)
- **Sell assets**: convert assets (property, equipment) to cash (one-off, reduces capacity)

## Substantive content

**Sources of finance for growth.** *Retained profit* (Greggs uses this for store rollouts) is free and avoids ownership dilution but is limited by recent profits. *Share capital* (Tesla 2021, Cineworld 2024) raises huge sums but dilutes control and brings public scrutiny. *Loan capital* keeps ownership but interest reduces profit and may breach covenants. *Selling assets* (Cineworld) is a quick fix in distress but reduces future revenue capacity.

**Profit and margins (2.4.1).** Gross profit measures how efficiently the firm produces; net profit measures overall profitability after all costs. **Margins** are essential because comparing absolute profit between firms of different sizes is misleading — Greggs' £100m profit on £1.5bn sales (≈6.7%) vs a small café's £20k on £100k sales (20%) shows the café is more efficient per £ of sales. Improving GP margin = raise prices, cut cost of sales, improve product mix. Improving NP margin = also cut overheads (rent, salaries, marketing).

**Average rate of return (2.4.1).** Compares investment options. ARR (%) = (average annual profit / cost of investment) × 100. *June 2024 worked example*: total profit £500,000 over 5 years; cost £200,000 → average annual profit = £100,000 → ARR = (100,000 / 200,000) × 100 = **50%**. The higher the ARR, the more attractive the investment — but ARR ignores when cash arrives and ignores risk.

**Interpreting and limitations.** Financial data should be *compared* — to last year, to industry average, to a competitor. Limits: data is historic; ignores qualitative factors (brand, staff morale); profit isn't cash (a profitable firm can fail if cash flow collapses).

> [!tip] 2 d.p. rule
> Margin questions on Paper 2 routinely demand answers **to 2 decimal places** (June 2019 GP margin = 72.61%, June 2023 NP margin = 44.44%). Read the wording carefully and check your rounding.

## Common exam pitfalls
- Forgetting × 100 on margin questions
- Computing total profit instead of *average annual profit* on ARR
- Treating cash flow as profit
- Not stating units / % sign
