# 1.1 Enterprise and entrepreneurship

> [!info] Spec reference
> Topic 1.1 covers why and how new business ideas come about (1.1.1 Dynamic nature of business), the impact of risk and reward on business activity (1.1.2), and the role of business enterprise and the entrepreneur (1.1.3).

## What this topic covers
- Why new business ideas come about: changes in technology, changes in consumer wants, products/services becoming obsolete
- How new business ideas come about: original ideas vs adapting existing ones (e.g. taking an existing café and adding a delivery angle)
- The concept of risk (financial loss, lack of security, business failure) vs reward (profit, independence, personal satisfaction)
- The role of business enterprise: producing goods/services, meeting customer needs, adding value
- The role of the entrepreneur: organising resources, making business decisions, taking risks

## Key terms
- **Enterprise**: the act of identifying a business opportunity and taking the risk to pursue it
- **Entrepreneur**: an individual who organises resources, takes financial risk and runs a business
- **Risk**: the possibility of loss or business failure (financial loss, time, reputation)
- **Reward**: the benefits of running a business (profit, independence, satisfaction)
- **Obsolete**: no longer wanted/used because something better has replaced it (e.g. DVD rentals)
- **Original idea**: an entirely new product or service no other business offers
- **Adapted idea**: improving or modifying an existing product/service
- **Adding value**: increasing the worth of a product so customers pay more than the cost of inputs
- **USP (Unique Selling Point)**: a feature that makes a product different from competitors
- **Dynamic market**: a market that changes rapidly (e.g. smartphones, fashion)

## Substantive content

**Dynamic nature of business (1.1.1).** Markets constantly change because consumer tastes shift (e.g. growth of plant-based food drove Greggs' vegan sausage roll), technology evolves (streaming made DVD rentals obsolete), and demographics shift. Businesses must spot these changes early. Examples: Netflix adapted from DVD posting to streaming; Tesla spotted EV demand before legacy carmakers.

**Risk and reward (1.1.2).** Every entrepreneur weighs trade-offs. Risks include: business failure (around 60% of UK start-ups fail within 5 years), losing personal savings, unlimited liability for sole traders, stress and long hours. Rewards include: profit, being your own boss, personal satisfaction from building something, the chance to scale (e.g. Zoella started as a blog and became a beauty brand). Higher risk often correlates with higher potential reward — investing all savings into a niche product can yield big returns or wipe out the entrepreneur.

**Role of the entrepreneur (1.1.3).** Three core functions: (1) **organising resources** — bringing together land, labour and capital; (2) **making business decisions** — what to sell, where, at what price; (3) **taking risks** — putting personal money on the line and accepting they may lose it. Real example: Adikoggz (June 2024) started as a hobby customising trainers; the entrepreneur took the risk of turning it into a business, sourcing materials, choosing prices, and managing all decisions personally.

> [!example] Application tip
> When the case study names the founder (e.g. "Amelia at Lili Heating"), every Justify/Evaluate answer should reference *her* personal motivations, *her* risk appetite, and *her* skills — not a generic "the owner".

## Common exam pitfalls
- Confusing "risk" with "uncertainty" — risk is measurable likelihood of loss; uncertainty is unknown
- Generic answers on "Explain one risk…" — name the actual case-study business and the specific risk it faces
- Forgetting that **reward is not just money** — independence and personal satisfaction are valid
