# Chart and Graph Skills

> [!info] Why this matters
> Every paper presents at least one chart in Section A and one or more in Sections B/C. **Identify** (1 mark) and **Calculate-from-chart** (2 marks) questions are guaranteed. Misreading the chart loses easy marks the rest of the cohort wins.

## Chart types you must recognise

### 1. Bar-gate (re-order) stock graph — **Paper 2 most-tested**
A "saw-tooth" graph showing stock falling at a constant rate then jumping up when a delivery arrives.
- **Buffer stock**: the horizontal floor line — stock should never drop below this
- **Re-order level**: the value at which a new order is placed (stock has fallen part-way down)
- **Lead time**: the horizontal gap between placing the order and the delivery arriving
- **Maximum stock**: the value immediately after a delivery
- **Order size (delivery quantity)** = max stock − buffer stock
- **Days out of stock**: any flat segment along the x-axis at zero (KFC Nov 2020: 4 days)

> [!warning] Common pitfall
> When asked "size of the order delivered", read **(max stock − buffer stock)**, NOT max stock. Examiners explicitly flag this in the Nov 2020 examiner report.

See [[Paper 2 Bar-Gate Stock Graphs]] for full diagram-style walkthrough.

### 2. Break-even diagram — Paper 1
Three lines on one chart: total revenue (sloping up from origin), total cost (sloping up from fixed-cost intercept), fixed cost (horizontal).
- **Break-even point**: where TR crosses TC — read down to x-axis = BEP units, read across = BEP £
- **Profit area**: shaded between TR and TC to the right of BEP
- **Loss area**: shaded between TC and TR to the left of BEP
- **Margin of safety**: horizontal distance between actual sales and BEP units

The Specimen Set 1 paper specifically asked students to **shade the profit region**.

### 3. Pie / doughnut chart — market share
Each slice = one segment's share. Sum of slices = 100%.
- **Identify largest/smallest segment** = name the label of the biggest/smallest slice
- **Calculate residual %** = 100 − (sum of given %s) [Nov 2021 Tesla market share = 8.2%]
- **Read a single slice** — read the % label on it
- A doughnut chart works the same way (Spec Set 2 used a doughnut for EV market share)

### 4. Bar chart (vertical and horizontal)
Most common chart format — used for revenue, costs, units, market share over time.
- **Read individual bars** off the y-axis scale
- **Compare** consecutive bars for trends (rising, falling, peaks)
- **Calculate total** by summing bar heights × scale (SAMs Q3b: 3,000 + 5,000 + 4,000 units × £500)
- **Identify highest/lowest** by spotting the tallest/shortest bar

### 5. Line graph — trends over time
Used for share price (ASOS 2021), interest rates (June 2019), cash-flow trend.
- Read the **y-value at a specific x-point** (e.g. share price in 2018)
- Identify **points where the line crosses a threshold** ("identify year share price > £70")
- Spot **trends** (upward/downward/seasonal)

### 6. Tabular data
Every paper has at least one table (Table 1 in Section A, often Table 2 in Section B).
- Add or subtract rows for cash flow / GP / NP
- Sum a row for revenue
- Compare across columns for year-on-year change

## Common pitfalls across chart types

> [!warning] Three repeat mistakes from examiner reports
> 1. **Misreading the scale** — bar chart in £000s or in millions; always check the axis label
> 2. **Reading the wrong year/segment** — point at the right bar with your finger before writing
> 3. **Forgetting to label the answer** — write "Tesco (27.8%)" not just "Tesco" if % is asked

## Reading checklist (apply before answering ANY chart question)

1. Read the **chart title** and **axis labels** in full
2. Note the **scale** (units, £, %, thousands)
3. Note the **time period** if shown
4. Underline the **specific value/segment** the question asks about
5. Show your read in workings if the question requires a calculation
